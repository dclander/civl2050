The Navier–Stokes equations

The equation is Newton's second law written for a fluid. The left side is the acceleration field from the previous class, multiplied by density.

ρDVDt = −∇p + μ∇2V + ρg DVDt = ∂V∂t + (V·∇)V

Reading the terms

ρ DV/Dt Mass per unit volume times the acceleration of a fluid particle. This is the quantity computed for the street-sign field. −∇p Net pressure force per unit volume. Pressure pushes from high to low, which is the source of the minus sign. μ∇2V Net viscous force per unit volume. Proportional to the curvature of the velocity profile. ρg Body force per unit volume, gravity in this course.

Status of each side

The right side is a sum of forces per unit volume. The left side is mass per unit volume times acceleration. The equation states F = ma for a differential element of fluid.

Three unknown velocity components and pressure make four unknowns. The three components of this equation plus conservation of mass give four equations.

Component by component

∂p/∂x = 0 ∂p/∂y = 0 dp/dz = −ρg

With z measured upward. Pressure is constant in any horizontal plane and varies only with depth.

What was used in Classes 02 to 04

Integrating dp/dz = −ρg for constant density gives p = ρgh, the linear pressure distribution used for manometry, plane surfaces, curved surfaces and buoyancy.

The viscous term vanishes in statics regardless of viscosity, because it acts on velocity gradients and there is no velocity. Water and honey have identical pressure distributions at rest.

Three assumptions reduce the equation to the form used in Class 07. Steady flow, no viscosity, constant density, integrated along a streamline.

ρ( ∂u/∂t + u∂u/∂x + v∂u/∂y + w∂u/∂z ) = −∂p/∂x + μ∇2u + ρgx p + ½ρV2 + ρgz = constant along a streamline

What each assumption removes

  • Steady. ∂/∂t = 0 removes the local acceleration.
  • Inviscid. μ = 0 removes the viscous term. This is the assumption that fails near solid surfaces.
  • Incompressible. ρ constant allows it to be taken inside the derivatives during integration.
  • Along a streamline. The convective term integrates to ½ρV2 only along the direction of motion.

Where the kinetic term comes from

Along a streamline the convective group reduces to V dV/ds, and V dV = d(½V2). Integrating gives the ½ρV2 term.

The pressure gradient integrates to p and the body force to ρgz. Bernoulli is the Navier–Stokes equation with two terms deleted and the rest integrated once.

Two terms compete. The convective term concentrates velocity; the viscous term spreads it out. The open question is whether concentration can win completely in finite time.

Peak speed against time for two possible behaviours, starting from the same smooth initial condition.

The two outcomes

Regularity The peak speed stays finite for all time. The solution remains smooth and the equation keeps predicting. Blowup The peak speed reaches infinity at a finite time T. Beyond T the equation has no solution in the usual sense.

Why it is not obvious

Stretching a vortex tube conserves its angular momentum, so thinning the tube speeds up its rotation. That is the concentrating mechanism, and it is contained in the convective term.

Viscosity acts against it, and acts more strongly at smaller scales. Which one dominates as the scale shrinks is not settled by any known argument in three dimensions. In two dimensions vortex stretching does not occur and regularity is proved.

A Burgers vortex is the standard picture of three effects in balance: axial straining, inward convection of vorticity, and outward viscous diffusion. The core settles at a fixed radius. The 2026 construction is the case where the balance fails.

Burgers vortex

An exact steady solution of the Navier–Stokes equations. The outer flow is a uniform straining field that carries vorticity inward while viscosity spreads it back out.

ur = −ar/2 , uz = az

This is three-dimensional, not planar. In Cartesian components it is (−ax/2, −ay/2, az): equal contraction in both transverse directions at rate a/2, extension along the axis at rate a. There is no z in ur, so the inflow is radial at every height, and uz = az vanishes on the plane z = 0, which is where the outflow divides.

r2z = constantmeridional streamlines

That is the axisymmetric counterpart of xy = C from the street-sign problem. The exponent differs only because the contraction is shared between two transverse directions instead of one.

δ = 2√(ν/a)

The core radius is fixed by the balance and does not change with time. The Burgers vortex is a line vortex, unbounded in z; the length of the tube drawn here is a drawing choice and carries no physics. Only δ is set by the equations.

The collapsing case

With a smooth forcing term supplying circulation, the inward transport outruns the outward diffusion. The core radius is no longer fixed. Writing τ = 1 − t, with h < 1/100:

core radius δ ~ τ1/2 core height ~ τ1/2−h swirl speed uθ ~ τ−1/2−h peak vorticity uθ/δ ~ τ−1−h circulation Γ ~ τ−h core energy ~ τ1/2−3h

Radius and height carry almost the same exponent, so the region shrinks in all three directions at once and keeps its shape. That is the scaling of the equations themselves: the Navier–Stokes equations are invariant under x → λx, t → λ2t, u → u/λ, and an exactly self-similar blow-up concentrates on lengths τ1/2 in every direction. The exponent h is the departure from that.

The shrinking box is a region of space, not a lump of fluid. Fluid is stretched along the axis and leaves through the ends the whole time. What contracts is the region in which the vorticity is concentrated.

uθ grows faster than δ shrinks, so the vorticity in the core diverges. A singularity requires exactly that.

The speed diverges while the kinetic energy in the core tends to zero, because the volume shrinks faster than the speed grows. The circulation grows, which a freely evolving vortex cannot do. That is what the forcing term supplies, and it is why the result applies to the forced equations only.

The steady Burgers vortex in three dimensions

The full velocity field, with δ = 2√(ν/a):

ur = −ar/2 , uz = az uθ = Γ2πr [1 − e−r2/δ2]

The field is steady, so streamlines, pathlines and streaklines coincide. Because uθ is present, those lines are helices, not the plane curves of the meridional view. Outside the core uθ ≈ Γ/2πr and the angular rate uθ/r goes as 1/r2; inside it the bracket ≈ r2/δ2 and the angular rate is constant, so the core turns as a solid body.

Radius and height on the figure are in units of δ, time in units of 1/a. Radial position follows r = r0e−at/2 and axial position z = z0eat exactly. The swirl strength is set to Γ/2π = 10aδ2.

Particle path

One particle released far from the axis, close to the plane z = 0. It is drawn inward at an exponential rate, and its angular rate rises as 1/r2, so the turns become tighter as it approaches the core. At the same time uz = az carries it away from the midplane, exponentially. The curve traced is a helix of decreasing radius and increasing pitch. Nothing about the vortex itself is changing; the flow is steady.

Material line

A straight line of dyed fluid laid out radially at t = 0, shown dashed, and the same fluid particles at the elapsed time. Every particle on the line rotates at its own rate, faster at smaller r, so the line is wound into a spiral. Its length grows without bound while the fluid it is made of stays the same. The whole line also rises, because uz depends only on z.

Vortex lines

In this flow the vorticity is purely axial:

ωz = Γπδ2e−r2/δ2

so the vortex lines are straight and parallel to the axis. They are carried with the fluid: each is pulled inward and stretched along z. A segment of length L becomes Leat while the bundle's cross-section shrinks as e−at, so the flux through it is unchanged and ω grows in the same proportion as the length. This is the term ω·∇u in the vorticity equation, drawn rather than written. It is the only mechanism by which vorticity can be amplified, and it is what viscous diffusion opposes.

Facts as of 15 September 2026.

What was proved

  • For every viscosity ν > 0 there exists a smooth, compactly supported forcing term f and a solution on ℝ3 × [0, 1) with zero initial velocity.
  • The solution has bounded kinetic energy at all times and its peak speed tends to infinity as t → 1.
  • The forcing term is part of the construction, not an artefact. The result does not apply to the unforced equations.

What remains open

  • The Clay Millennium problem concerns the unforced equations. That case is unresolved.
  • The Clay Mathematics Institute recorded the status as active on 11 September 2026 and requires peer-reviewed publication before any award.
  • A Lean formalisation has been released. Independent verification is not complete.
